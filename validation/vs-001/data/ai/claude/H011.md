# How We Made the Atlassian Design Guidelines

When we set out to create a comprehensive design system for our suite of products, we knew we were embarking on one of the most important initiatives in our company's history. The Atlassian Design Guidelines didn't emerge overnight—they were the product of extensive research, collaboration, and a deep commitment to creating better experiences for teams like yours.

Today, we're pulling back the curtain to share how we built these guidelines, why they matter for your team, and what they mean for the products you use every day.

## The Problem We Needed to Solve

A few years ago, we faced a challenge that many growing companies encounter: our products were evolving rapidly, but inconsistently. Jira looked different from Confluence. Confluence felt different from Trello. Each product team was making brilliant decisions in isolation, but the cumulative effect created a fragmented experience for our customers.

We heard from teams who used multiple Atlassian products that they were essentially learning different "languages" for each tool. A button in one product didn't quite match a button in another. Navigation patterns varied. The experience of moving between our products felt jarring rather than seamless.

This wasn't just an aesthetic concern—it was a productivity problem. Every moment your team spends figuring out how a different Atlassian product works is a moment they're not spending on their actual work. We realized that if we truly wanted to unleash the potential of every team, we needed to create a unified design language that would make our entire ecosystem feel cohesive, intuitive, and powerful.

## Building the Foundation: Research and Discovery

We started where we always start: by listening to you, our customers.

Our research team conducted hundreds of interviews with teams across different industries, company sizes, and use cases. We visited offices, joined remote calls, and observed how real teams interacted with our products throughout their workday. We analyzed usage data, support tickets, and user feedback to identify pain points and friction areas.

What we discovered was enlightening. Teams told us they valued consistency above almost everything else. They wanted to build muscle memory that would transfer across products. They wanted design patterns that were predictable, accessible, and efficient. Most importantly, they wanted our tools to fade into the background so they could focus on their work, not on figuring out how to use our software.

Armed with these insights, we assembled a cross-functional team that included designers, developers, product managers, researchers, and accessibility experts. We knew that creating truly great design guidelines would require diverse perspectives and expertise.

## Establishing Core Principles

Before we could design a single component or write a line of code, we needed to establish the principles that would guide every decision. These principles became our North Star—the criteria against which we would evaluate every element of the design system.

We landed on four core principles:

**Bold, Optimistic, and Practical.** Our products serve teams doing important work, and our design should reflect the significance of that work while remaining grounded and usable.

**Designed for Scale.** Whether you're a team of five or five thousand, our products should adapt to your needs without feeling overwhelming or overly simplistic.

**Accessible to All.** Great design isn't just beautiful—it's inclusive. We committed to meeting and exceeding accessibility standards from day one.

**Coherent Across Products.** Learning one Atlassian product should make learning another easier, not harder.

These principles weren't just marketing speak—they became the practical framework for every design decision we made.

## Designing the Visual Language

With our principles established, we began the detailed work of creating our visual language. This meant making decisions about everything from color palettes and typography to spacing systems and iconography.

Our color system was particularly challenging. We needed colors that would work across multiple products with different purposes, remain accessible for users with visual impairments, and feel modern without being trendy. We tested hundreds of color combinations, ran accessibility audits, and gathered feedback from our design community.

The result was a carefully crafted palette built on a foundation of blue—a color associated with trust and professionalism—complemented by a range of supporting colors that could convey different states, emotions, and types of information. Every color was tested to ensure it met WCAG AA standards for contrast, and many meet the more stringent AAA standard.

Typography required similar rigor. We evaluated dozens of typefaces before selecting ones that were highly legible on screens of all sizes, performed well across different operating systems and browsers, and could handle the diverse types of content our products display—from code snippets to lengthy documentation.

## Creating Reusable Components

The heart of any design system is its component library—the reusable building blocks that designers and developers use to build interfaces. We knew that creating a comprehensive, flexible component library would be essential to achieving consistency across our products.

We started by auditing every interface element across all Atlassian products. We catalogued buttons, forms, tables, navigation menus, modals, and dozens of other components. Then we asked ourselves: which variations are truly necessary, and which exist simply because different teams solved the same problem in different ways?

This consolidation process was both liberating and challenging. We reduced eight different button styles down to three well-defined variants. We unified form controls that had evolved differently across products. We created navigation patterns that could scale from simple to complex use cases.

Each component was designed with flexibility in mind. A button, for instance, needed to work in dozens of contexts—in a crowded toolbar, at the end of a form, as a standalone call-to-action, or within a compact mobile interface. We created systematic variations that covered these use cases without creating endless one-off solutions.

## Prioritizing Accessibility

Accessibility wasn't an afterthought—it was integrated into every stage of the design process. We worked with accessibility consultants, conducted testing with users who rely on assistive technologies, and educated our entire team on inclusive design practices.

We established clear guidelines for color contrast, keyboard navigation, screen reader compatibility, and focus management. We created components that worked seamlessly with assistive technologies and included features like skip links, proper heading hierarchies, and ARIA labels by default.

This commitment to accessibility benefits all users, not just those with disabilities. Clear visual hierarchies, consistent navigation, and thoughtful interaction design make our products easier for everyone to use.

## Documentation and Education

Creating the design guidelines was only half the battle—we also needed to ensure that teams across Atlassian could actually use them. This required comprehensive documentation and extensive education efforts.

We built a dedicated website that serves as the single source of truth for our design system. Every component is documented with usage guidelines, code examples, accessibility considerations, and best practices. We included do's and don't's illustrated with real examples, making it easy for designers and developers to make good decisions quickly.

But documentation alone wasn't enough. We created training programs, held workshops, and established office hours where team members could get help implementing the guidelines. We built tools and plugins for popular design software that made it easy to use approved components and patterns.

## Iteration and Evolution

One of the most important lessons we learned is that design guidelines are never truly "finished." They need to evolve as technology changes, user needs shift, and new patterns emerge.

We established a governance model that allows the design system to grow and adapt while maintaining consistency. We created processes for proposing new components, requesting modifications to existing ones, and deprecating patterns that are no longer serving users well.

We also committed to regular updates based on feedback from both our internal teams and, most importantly, from customers like you. When we hear that something isn't working well or could be improved, we investigate and iterate.

## What This Means for Your Team

You might be wondering: why should I, as an Atlassian customer, care about all this behind-the-scenes work?

The Atlassian Design Guidelines directly impact your daily experience with our products. They mean that when you switch from Jira to Confluence, you'll find familiar patterns that reduce the learning curve. They mean that our products are more accessible to all members of your team. They mean that new features and updates feel cohesive rather than tacked on.

More fundamentally, they represent our commitment to craft—to sweating the details that make software a pleasure to use rather than a chore to endure. They're our promise that we're building products that respect your time and intelligence.

## Looking Forward

The creation of the Atlassian Design Guidelines marked a pivotal moment in our company's evolution, but it's just the beginning. We continue to refine and expand these guidelines as we build new products, enter new markets, and respond to your feedback.

We're also sharing what we've learned with the broader design community, contributing to conversations about design systems, accessibility, and product design. We believe that improving the practice of design benefits everyone.

For your team, this ongoing commitment means that Atlassian products will continue to become more intuitive, more consistent, and more powerful. Every update, every new feature, and every product enhancement is built on the foundation of these design guidelines.

The hundreds of hours of research, thousands of design iterations, and countless conversations that went into creating the Atlassian Design Guidelines were all in service of one goal: helping your team do their best work with tools that empower rather than obstruct.

That's the promise of thoughtful, principled design—and it's a promise we're committed to keeping.
