# How Intercom Got Our First Customer

Getting your first customers is one of the most challenging—and exhilarating—experiences in building a business. At Intercom, our journey from idea to first paying customers taught us lessons that still shape how we build products and serve our customers today.

We're sharing our story not just as a trip down memory lane, but because we believe the principles that helped us gain early traction can help you think differently about acquiring and serving your own customers.

## Starting With a Problem We Actually Had

In 2011, before Intercom existed, our founding team had a frustrating problem. We'd all worked at various tech companies and startups, and we kept running into the same issue: we had no idea who our users actually were or what they were doing in our products.

We'd see an email address in our database, maybe a signup date, but that was about it. Were they active? What features did they use? What problems were they trying to solve? We were flying blind.

This wasn't just an abstract annoyance—it was costing real money. Customer support teams couldn't help users effectively. Sales teams couldn't identify hot leads. Product teams were building features based on gut feelings rather than real user behavior.

So we started building a tool for ourselves. A simple way to see who your users are, what they do in your product, and talk to them at the right moment. We didn't set out to start a customer messaging platform—we set out to solve our own painful problem.

**The lesson:** The best first customers often look exactly like you. If you're building something to solve your own problem, there are likely hundreds or thousands of others experiencing the same frustration.

## Building in Public Before It Was Cool

While we were building our initial prototype, we did something that felt risky at the time: we started talking about what we were creating before it was finished.

Our co-founder Des Traynor began writing blog posts about the problem of knowing your users, the importance of customer engagement, and our philosophy on building products. These weren't promotional posts—they were thoughtful essays about the challenges we were wrestling with.

Something unexpected happened. People started reaching out. Not asking for our product (which wasn't ready yet), but sharing their own struggles with the same problems. They'd comment on the posts, send emails, and share the articles with their colleagues.

By the time we had something to show, we'd already built an audience of people who understood exactly what we were trying to solve. They got it immediately because we'd been having a conversation with them for months.

**The lesson:** Your prospective customers don't want to be sold to—they want to be understood. Start the conversation before you have something to sell. Share your thinking, your challenges, and your insights about the problem space.

## The Invitation-Only Launch

When we finally had a working version of Intercom, we didn't throw open the doors to everyone. Instead, we launched with an invitation-only beta in August 2011.

This wasn't because we wanted to be exclusive for the sake of it. We knew our product wasn't perfect yet, and we wanted to work closely with a small group of customers who would give us honest feedback and help us shape what we were building.

We reached out to about 20 companies we'd connected with through our blog and our network. These were tech companies, mostly startups, who we knew struggled with the same user communication challenges we'd experienced.

The pitch was simple: "We've built something we think can help you know your users better and communicate with them more effectively. It's not finished, but we'd love your help making it better."

Thirteen of those twenty companies said yes. Those thirteen became our first customers, even though we were barely charging anything at that point.

**The lesson:** Your first customers aren't really buying your product—they're buying into your vision and betting on your team. Choose customers who can be partners in building something great, not just users of a finished product.

## Learning to Listen (Really Listen)

Having those first customers could have gone one of two ways. We could have taken their money and disappeared into our development cave, or we could have truly partnered with them.

We chose partnership.

We set up regular calls with each early customer. Not support calls or check-ins, but real conversations about what they were trying to accomplish, what was working, and what wasn't. Des would often spend an hour on a call with a single customer, just understanding their business.

One early customer, a project management software company, told us they loved seeing user data, but they were drowning in it. They didn't need more information—they needed to know what to do with it. That feedback directly led to us building our targeting and segmentation features.

Another customer mentioned they felt awkward sending messages to users because it felt like spam. That conversation sparked our thinking about contextual, in-app messaging that feels helpful rather than interruptive.

These weren't feature requests we checked off a list. They were insights that shaped our product philosophy.

**The lesson:** Early customer conversations aren't about collecting feature requests—they're about understanding the deeper problems your customers are trying to solve. The best product improvements come from understanding context, not just complaints.

## Charging Money From Day One (Almost)

We made a decision early on that surprised some people: we charged for Intercom almost from the beginning.

Even during our beta period, we asked customers to pay something—even if it was just $49 per month. It wasn't about the revenue (thirteen customers at $49 each wasn't going to fund the business). It was about the relationship.

When someone pays you money, even a small amount, the relationship changes. They become more invested. They care more about whether it works. And critically, they tell you the truth about whether it's actually valuable.

Free users will tolerate mediocre products. Paying customers won't. They'll tell you exactly what needs to improve because they have skin in the game.

We did have some completely free beta users early on, and we noticed the difference immediately. Paid customers gave us detailed feedback. They told us when things broke. They suggested improvements. Many of our free users just disappeared without explanation.

**The lesson:** Charge for your product as early as possible. It validates that you're solving a real problem, and it creates customers who are invested in your success.

## Turning Customers Into Champions

Our first customers didn't just use Intercom—many of them became advocates for it.

This didn't happen by accident. We made our early customers feel like insiders, because they were. We shared our product roadmap with them. We named features after their suggestions. We told them about challenges we were facing and asked for their advice.

When we asked one of our beta customers if they'd be willing to share their experience using Intercom, they didn't just say yes—they introduced us to three other companies in their network.

We learned that people love being part of building something. When you invite customers into your journey and make them feel like partners rather than transactions, they naturally want to tell others about what you're creating together.

Several of our first twenty customers are still with us today, over a decade later. They've grown from small startups to substantial companies, and we've grown alongside them.

**The lesson:** Your first customers can become your best marketing channel, but only if you make them feel like valued partners. Invest in those relationships like they're the most important thing in the world—because at the beginning, they are.

## What We'd Do Differently

Looking back, we'd change a few things.

We probably could have charged more earlier. At $49 per month, we were undervaluing what we offered, even in those early days. Higher prices would have helped us focus on customers who saw real value in what we were building.

We also could have been more systematic about capturing feedback. In those early days, insights from customer conversations lived in people's heads or scattered emails. Building a better system for collecting and analyzing customer feedback would have helped us spot patterns faster.

But overall, the approach worked because it was built on genuine relationships and solving real problems—not growth hacks or marketing tricks.

## Your First Customers Are Out There

If you're working on getting your first customers, remember this: they're not looking for perfection. They're looking for someone who understands their problem and is committed to solving it.

Start conversations before you have something to sell. Build in public. Find customers who can be partners. Listen deeply to what they're really trying to accomplish. And charge money to validate that you're creating real value.

Your first customers won't just give you revenue—they'll give you the insights, credibility, and momentum you need to build something that matters.

That's exactly what ours did for us.
