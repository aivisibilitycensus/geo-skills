# 5 Ways to Attract Women to Your Tech Company

The technology industry has a well-documented gender gap. Despite comprising roughly half the workforce across all industries, women represent only about 28% of the tech sector—and even fewer in technical roles like software engineering and data science. This isn't just a fairness issue; it's a business problem. Companies with diverse teams consistently outperform their homogeneous counterparts in innovation, problem-solving, and financial returns.

If you're struggling to attract female talent to your tech company, you're not alone—but you also can't afford to accept the status quo. The good news? With intentional strategies and genuine commitment, you can make your organization more appealing to women in tech. Let's explore five proven approaches that will help you build a more diverse, innovative, and successful team.

## 1. Rewrite Your Job Descriptions to Eliminate Unconscious Bias

Your job posting is often the first interaction a candidate has with your company, and it might be quietly turning away qualified women before they even apply. Research has consistently shown that gendered language in job descriptions significantly impacts application rates—and tech companies are among the worst offenders.

### The Problem with "Aggressive" Language

Studies reveal that women are less likely to apply for positions that use traditionally masculine-coded language. Words like "aggressive," "dominant," "competitive," "ninja," "rockstar," and "guru" may seem harmless or even exciting, but they create subtle signals about company culture that can discourage female applicants. Meanwhile, words like "collaborative," "supportive," "responsible," and "committed" tend to appeal more broadly across genders without deterring male candidates.

Beyond individual word choices, many tech job postings fall into another trap: the laundry list of requirements. Research from Hewlett Packard found that men apply for jobs when they meet just 60% of qualifications, while women typically apply only when they meet 100% of them. If your job description demands five years of experience when three would suffice, or lists "nice-to-have" skills alongside genuine requirements, you're likely screening out talented women who would excel in the role.

### How to Fix Your Job Descriptions

Start by running your job postings through textual analysis tools designed to identify gendered language. Several free and paid options can flag problematic wording and suggest alternatives. Beyond language, review your actual requirements critically. Separate "must-haves" from "nice-to-haves," and be honest about which qualifications are truly necessary for success.

Consider including explicit encouragement for diverse candidates to apply, such as: "Studies have shown that women and people of color are less likely to apply for jobs unless they meet every qualification. We encourage you to apply even if you don't meet all the listed requirements—we're committed to building a diverse team and recognize that talent comes from many backgrounds."

Finally, highlight what you offer, not just what you demand. Women in tech report caring deeply about company mission, growth opportunities, and work-life balance. Make these elements prominent in your job descriptions rather than burying them at the bottom.

## 2. Implement Structured, Bias-Resistant Hiring Processes

Even with perfectly crafted job descriptions, unconscious bias can infiltrate your hiring process at every stage—from resume screening to interviews to final decisions. Creating structure around your hiring process is one of the most effective ways to ensure women are evaluated fairly.

### Standardize Your Interview Process

Unstructured interviews—where different candidates are asked different questions and evaluated on different criteria—are breeding grounds for bias. Interviewers tend to ask questions that confirm their initial impressions rather than challenge them, and without clear evaluation criteria, subjective "gut feelings" often determine outcomes.

Instead, develop a standardized interview framework for each role. This means asking every candidate the same core questions, using evidence-based interviewing techniques like structured behavioral interviews and work sample tests, and establishing clear rubrics for evaluating responses. When everyone is measured against the same yardstick, bias has less room to operate.

### Diversify Your Interview Panels

Who conducts your interviews matters just as much as how they're conducted. Research shows that candidates from underrepresented groups perform better and perceive the process as more fair when they see people like themselves among the interviewers. If your interview panels are entirely male, female candidates receive a clear signal about whether they'll fit in at your company.

Make diversity on interview panels a standard practice, not an afterthought. If your organization is still in the early stages of building gender diversity, consider including women from other departments or bringing in female advisors or board members for final-round conversations. This demonstrates your commitment to diversity even while you're still building it.

### Remove Identifying Information from Resume Reviews

"Blind" resume screening—removing names and other identifying information before initial reviews—has shown significant promise in reducing bias. Multiple studies have demonstrated that identical resumes with stereotypically male names receive more callbacks than those with female names, particularly in technical fields.

Modern applicant tracking systems can facilitate blind screening by hiding candidate names and other potentially identifying details during initial reviews. While blind screening isn't a complete solution (and should be combined with other strategies), it helps ensure that candidates advance based on qualifications rather than assumptions.

## 3. Showcase Women Leaders and Employees Authentically

Representation matters. When prospective candidates explore your company, they're looking for signals about whether they'll belong, thrive, and advance. If your website, social media, and marketing materials show only men in leadership positions and technical roles, you're answering that question before women even apply.

### Make Diversity Visible Across All Channels

Audit your company's public-facing presence—your website, LinkedIn profile, social media accounts, blog posts, conference presentations, and marketing materials. Who appears in photos? Who writes content? Who represents your company at industry events? If women are absent or relegated to supporting roles in these materials, you're communicating that they hold those same positions in your actual organization.

This doesn't mean staging inauthentic photos or tokenizing the women you do employ. Instead, make a conscious effort to highlight the women who are doing excellent work at your company. Feature female engineers in technical blog posts. Include women leaders in conference panels and podcast interviews. Show women in both collaborative settings and positions of authority.

### Share Authentic Stories from Women Employees

Beyond surface-level representation, share substantive stories from women at your company about their experiences, career growth, and meaningful work. These narratives help prospective candidates envision their own futures at your organization.

Consider creating employee spotlight series, career journey blog posts, or video testimonials from women across different roles and levels. Let them speak honestly about their experiences—including the challenges they've faced and how the company supported them. This authenticity is far more powerful than generic diversity statements.

### Highlight Women in Leadership

Women considering joining your company are evaluating not just whether they can get hired, but whether they can advance. If your leadership team and board are exclusively or predominantly male, that's valuable information—it suggests women may hit barriers as they try to move up.

Make investing in women's leadership development a genuine priority, and communicate these efforts transparently. Who's in your succession planning pipeline? What percentage of directors, VPs, and C-suite executives are women? How has this changed over time? Being honest about where you are while demonstrating clear commitment to progress builds more credibility than overstating your current state.

## 4. Offer Benefits and Policies That Support Work-Life Integration

Attracting women to your tech company isn't just about getting them in the door—it's about creating an environment where they'll want to stay and grow. The benefits and policies you offer send powerful signals about whether your organization values employees' whole lives or expects them to sacrifice everything for work.

### Go Beyond Standard Parental Leave

Parental leave policies are table stakes, but truly supportive companies go further. Offer generous, equitable parental leave for all parents regardless of gender—this supports women directly while also combating the assumption that only women take leave for caregiving. Companies that offer 12+ weeks of paid parental leave for all parents demonstrate genuine commitment to supporting employees through major life transitions.

But don't stop at leave. Consider phased return-to-work options, lactation support that goes beyond the legal minimum, backup childcare assistance, and clear policies protecting employees from discrimination related to pregnancy or parenting. Women in tech consistently report that these supports influence both their decision to join a company and their choice to stay.

### Embrace Flexible Work Arrangements

The pandemic proved that remote and flexible work arrangements are viable for many tech roles. Women—who still shoulder a disproportionate share of caregiving responsibilities—particularly value flexibility in where and when they work. Companies that insist on rigid office attendance or specific working hours without clear business justification are needlessly limiting their talent pool.

Implement flexible work policies that trust employees to manage their time effectively. This might include core collaboration hours with flexibility around when other work gets done, remote work options, or compressed work weeks. The key is ensuring these policies are actually used without career penalty—if flexibility is officially allowed but informally discouraged, it doesn't help.

### Support Professional Development and Growth

Women in tech frequently cite lack of advancement opportunities as a reason for leaving companies. Demonstrate your commitment to employee growth through structured professional development programs, clear promotion criteria, mentorship opportunities, and training budgets.

Consider implementing formal sponsorship programs where senior leaders actively advocate for high-potential women employees, helping them access challenging assignments, visibility, and advancement opportunities. Unlike mentorship, which provides advice, sponsorship involves leaders using their influence to open doors—and research shows it's particularly impactful for women's career advancement.

## 5. Create and Sustain an Inclusive Culture

All the recruiting strategies in the world won't help if women join your company only to encounter a hostile or exclusionary culture. Building genuine inclusion requires ongoing effort, accountability, and willingness to address problems when they arise.

### Establish Clear Values and Expected Behaviors

Culture isn't what you say—it's what you do and what you tolerate. Start by articulating clear values around diversity, equity, and inclusion, then translate those values into specific behavioral expectations. What does respectful communication look like in meetings? How should teams make decisions? What happens when someone witnesses bias or harassment?

Create clear reporting mechanisms for problems, and demonstrate through action that you take concerns seriously. When issues arise—and they will—how you respond matters more than maintaining an illusion of perfection. Companies that acknowledge problems and take corrective action build more trust than those that defensively deny any issues exist.

### Invest in Training and Education

Unconscious bias training has become common, but it's most effective as part of broader inclusion efforts rather than a one-time check-the-box exercise. Provide ongoing education about inclusive leadership, equitable decision-making, and recognizing bias. Make this training mandatory for all employees, especially managers and leaders who have the most influence over others' experiences.

Beyond bias training, educate your team about microaggressions, impostor syndrome, and other dynamics that particularly affect underrepresented employees. When everyone understands these concepts, it's easier to build a culture where they're actively addressed rather than ignored.

### Build Communities and Support Systems

Employee resource groups (ERGs) for women in tech can provide community, networking, and advocacy. Support these groups with budget, executive sponsorship, and genuine influence over company decisions. Women's ERGs at their best serve as both support systems for members and advisors to leadership on policies and practices that affect gender diversity.

Consider also creating structured peer support systems like women's leadership circles, technical mentorship programs that pair junior women engineers with senior women in the field (including outside your company if necessary), and networking opportunities with women in tech across the industry.

### Measure, Track, and Hold Leaders Accountable

What gets measured gets managed. Track gender diversity metrics across hiring, retention, promotion, and pay. Disaggregate data by level and department to identify where problems exist—you might have decent overall numbers but find women concentrated in non-technical roles or absent from senior leadership.

Make diversity outcomes part of how you evaluate leaders. If managers are responsible for building diverse teams, include that in their performance reviews and compensation decisions. When diversity becomes a business priority with real accountability, behavior changes.

## Moving Forward: Commitment Beyond Tactics

Attracting women to your tech company isn't about implementing a few clever recruiting tactics—it's about genuinely committing to building a workplace where women can thrive. The five strategies outlined above work best in combination, as parts of a comprehensive approach to diversity and inclusion.

Start by honestly assessing where your organization is today. Review your gender diversity numbers, audit your hiring processes for bias, examine your benefits and policies, and—most importantly—talk to the women already at your company about their experiences. They'll tell you what's working and what needs to change.

Then commit to sustained effort. Building gender diversity is a long-term project that requires ongoing attention, resources, and executive commitment. Quick fixes and superficial gestures won't work—and they'll likely backfire as word spreads that your diversity efforts are performative rather than genuine.

The tech industry's gender gap didn't develop overnight, and it won't be solved overnight. But every company that commits to these changes makes the industry more welcoming and equitable for everyone. The good news? The companies leading on gender diversity aren't just doing the right thing—they're also building competitive advantages in innovation, employee satisfaction, and business performance.

The women in tech are out there, talented and ready to contribute. The question is: will your company be ready to welcome them?
