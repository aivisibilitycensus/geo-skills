# The Duke Ellington Guide to Copy That Swings

Duke Ellington didn't just write music—he created experiences that moved people. His compositions had rhythm, soul, and an unmistakable voice that made you sit up and listen. Your business copy should do the same thing.

Just as Duke transformed simple melodies into unforgettable performances, great copywriting transforms ordinary messages into compelling stories that make customers take action. Let's explore what the maestro of swing can teach us about creating copy that resonates, persuades, and converts.

## It Don't Mean a Thing If It Ain't Got That Swing

Duke Ellington's most famous song gives us the first rule of powerful copy: rhythm matters.

Your customers are busy. They're scrolling, skimming, and making split-second decisions about what deserves their attention. Copy that swings has a natural flow that pulls readers along effortlessly. It varies sentence length. It uses short punchy statements. Then it stretches out with longer explanatory phrases that add depth and context before snapping back to something crisp.

Read your copy aloud. Does it have a rhythm? Do certain words feel clunky or awkward? Great copy, like great jazz, has an internal musicality that makes it easy to consume and hard to forget.

When Ellington composed, he knew exactly when to let a trumpet solo soar and when to bring in the full orchestra. Similarly, your copy needs to know when to make a bold claim and when to support it with detail. The rhythm creates momentum that carries your reader toward that crucial call-to-action.

## Take the 'A' Train—Give Clear Direction

"Take the 'A' Train," one of Ellington's signature pieces, offered straightforward advice: this is the fastest way to get to Harlem. Your copy needs the same clarity of purpose.

Every piece of content you create should have a clear destination. Where are you taking your reader? What action do you want them to take? Whether it's scheduling a consultation, downloading a resource, or making a purchase, your copy should provide unmistakable direction.

Confused customers don't convert. If your messaging tries to go everywhere at once, it ends up going nowhere. Like a train route, your copy should follow a logical path from attention to interest to desire to action.

Be specific. Instead of "Learn more," try "Download your free planning guide." Instead of "Contact us," try "Schedule your 15-minute strategy call." Give your readers the 'A' train, not a complicated subway map.

## It's Not the Notes, It's the Silence Between

Duke Ellington understood that the pauses in music were just as important as the notes. The same principle applies to copywriting—white space is your friend.

Dense walls of text intimidate readers. They signal effort, complexity, and time investment. Breaking up your copy with strategic spacing, subheadings, and bullet points makes it approachable and scannable.

Consider these formatting techniques:

- **Short paragraphs**: Keep them to 2-3 sentences for online reading
- **Subheadings**: Guide readers through your content like signposts
- **Bullet points**: Highlight key benefits or features (like this list)
- **Bold text**: Draw attention to critical phrases
- **Line breaks**: Give ideas room to breathe

The silence between the notes—the white space on your page—creates visual rhythm and makes your message easier to digest. It shows respect for your reader's time and cognitive load.

## Play to Your Band's Strengths

Duke Ellington wrote music specifically for the musicians in his orchestra. He knew Johnny Hodges' sultry alto sax sound and composed parts that showcased it. He understood each player's unique voice.

Your copy should do the same—play to your brand's strengths and speak in your authentic voice.

Are you the friendly, approachable expert? The premium, sophisticated choice? The innovative disruptor? The reliable, time-tested partner? Your tone should reflect your brand personality consistently across every touchpoint.

More importantly, know your audience. Ellington played differently at the Cotton Club than he did at Carnegie Hall—not because he compromised his artistry, but because he understood his audience. Your copy for a technical white paper should sound different than your social media posts, but both should unmistakably be *you*.

Authenticity resonates. Customers can spot corporate-speak and generic messaging from a mile away. Find your voice and use it confidently.

## Improvise, But Know Your Standards

Jazz musicians improvise brilliantly, but they build those improvisations on a foundation of standards—classic compositions everyone knows. Similarly, great copy balances creativity with proven structures.

Certain copywriting formulas work because they align with how people make decisions:

- **Problem-Agitate-Solve**: Identify the pain point, make it vivid, present your solution
- **Before-After-Bridge**: Show where they are, where they could be, and how to get there
- **Features-Advantages-Benefits**: Connect what you offer to why it matters

These are your standards—the classic structures that work. But within them, there's infinite room for creativity, personality, and differentiation. Use them as a foundation, then add your unique flourish.

## The Audience Makes the Performance

Duke Ellington once said, "A problem is a chance for you to do your best." He understood that performing wasn't about him—it was about creating an experience for the audience.

Your copy isn't about you either. It's about your customer—their challenges, their aspirations, their needs. The best copy focuses relentlessly on the reader, using "you" far more than "we."

Before writing anything, ask: What does my audience care about? What keeps them up at night? What would make their life better? Then address those concerns directly.
