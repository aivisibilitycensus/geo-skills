# 5 ways to cultivate inclusion in your organization

Building a diverse workforce is just the beginning. The real challenge—and opportunity—lies in creating an inclusive environment where every employee feels valued, heard, and empowered to do their best work. Organizations that prioritize inclusion don't just attract top talent; they retain it, innovate faster, and outperform their competitors.

At Greenhouse, we've seen firsthand how companies that embed inclusion into their DNA create stronger, more resilient teams. Here are five proven strategies to help you cultivate genuine inclusion in your organization.

## 1. Start with structured, equitable hiring practices

Inclusion begins before someone even joins your team. Your hiring process sets the tone for everything that follows, which is why implementing structured, bias-resistant practices is crucial.

Structured hiring means asking all candidates the same questions, evaluating them against consistent criteria, and ensuring multiple perspectives are included in hiring decisions. This approach reduces the unconscious biases that can creep into unstructured interviews and creates a more level playing field for all applicants.

Consider anonymizing resumes during initial screening to focus on skills and qualifications rather than names or educational pedigree. Build diverse interview panels that bring different perspectives to candidate evaluation. And most importantly, define what "good" looks like before you start reviewing applications—not after.

When candidates see a fair, transparent process, they're more likely to trust that your organization genuinely values inclusion. This foundation of equity from day one creates the conditions for an inclusive culture to flourish.

## 2. Create genuine belonging through inclusive onboarding

Your new hire's first few weeks will shape their entire experience at your company. An inclusive onboarding process helps employees from all backgrounds feel welcomed, connected, and set up for success.

Go beyond the standard paperwork and systems training. Pair new hires with onboarding buddies who can answer questions and provide cultural context. Create opportunities for them to build relationships across departments, not just within their immediate team. Share your organization's inclusion commitments and employee resource groups early so people know these resources exist.

Pay special attention to the unique needs of employees from underrepresented groups. Are remote workers getting the same face time and networking opportunities as in-office employees? Are international hires receiving support navigating cultural differences? Do employees with disabilities have the accommodations they need from day one?

Inclusive onboarding isn't about treating everyone identically—it's about ensuring everyone has what they need to thrive.

## 3. Foster psychological safety across all teams

Inclusion flourishes when people feel safe bringing their whole selves to work. Psychological safety—the belief that you won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes—is the bedrock of an inclusive culture.

Leaders play a critical role in creating psychological safety. Encourage managers to model vulnerability by acknowledging their own mistakes and uncertainties. Train them to actively solicit input from quieter team members and to create space for dissenting opinions. Recognize and reward employees who raise difficult questions or challenge the status quo constructively.

Create formal channels for feedback that allow employees to share concerns confidentially if needed. Regularly survey your teams about their sense of belonging and psychological safety, then act on what you learn. When people see that their feedback leads to real change, they're more likely to continue sharing openly.

Remember: psychological safety isn't about being nice or avoiding difficult conversations. It's about creating an environment where productive conflict and honest dialogue can happen without fear of retaliation.

## 4. Examine and redesign systems with an equity lens

True inclusion requires examining the systems and processes that govern how work gets done. Even well-intentioned organizations often have policies that inadvertently create barriers for certain groups.

Conduct regular equity audits of your key processes: How are performance reviews conducted, and who tends to receive top ratings? How are promotions and raises determined? Who has access to high-visibility projects and development opportunities? Are meeting schedules accessible for employees in different time zones or those with caregiving responsibilities?

Look at your benefits and policies through an inclusion lens. Do your health benefits adequately support LGBTQ+ employees? Does your parental leave policy support all types of families? Are your professional development budgets accessible to employees at all levels?

Sometimes the most significant barriers to inclusion are invisible to those who don't experience them. Actively seek input from employees with different identities and experiences, and empower them to help redesign systems that don't work.

## 5. Hold leaders accountable with meaningful metrics

What gets measured gets done. If you're serious about cultivating inclusion, you need to track your progress and hold leaders accountable for results.

Establish clear inclusion metrics that go beyond simple headcount diversity. Track retention rates across different demographic groups. Measure participation in employee resource groups and inclusion initiatives. Survey employees regularly about their sense of belonging and whether they feel their ideas are valued.

Most importantly, tie leadership performance and compensation to inclusion outcomes. When executives and managers know their success depends partly on building inclusive teams, inclusion becomes a business priority rather than an HR initiative.

Share your metrics transparently with the entire organization. Celebrate progress, but also acknowledge where you're falling short. This transparency builds trust and demonstrates that your commitment to inclusion is genuine and ongoing.

## Building inclusion is an ongoing journey

Cultivating inclusion isn't a one-time initiative—it's an ongoing commitment that requires constant attention, learning, and evolution. By implementing these five strategies, you'll create an organization where diverse talent doesn't just join, but stays, grows, and does their best work.

The organizations that win the talent war will be those where everyone truly belongs.
